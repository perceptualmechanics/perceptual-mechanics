Kichibei

Human · Artisan · Natural Philosopher 5 (Experimentalist) · level 5
PeopleEast and Southeast AsiaEdoExperimentalistLevel 5

Tempura came to Japan from Portuguese cooks at Nagasaki in the 1500s, and by the later 1600s Edo's street stalls fried fish and vegetables on skewers for porters and samurai alike; the shogunate forbade frying indoors for fear of fire. Nihonbashi, the bridge at the heart of Edo, is where the Tōkaidō and the other great roads begin.

Dusk at the foot of Nihonbashi, a stall of planks and a pot of oil hissing. A wiry man with a towel around his head lowers river fish in a pale batter, lifts them out gold, and hands them on skewers to a porter and a samurai standing side by side, which they would never do anywhere else. Between batches he scribbles in a notebook: the oil's heat, the batter's weight, the weather. He glances across the bridge, where another stall is selling fish that can't be in season, and his mouth goes thin.

Gear

A pot of sesame oil, a basket of river fish and vegetables, a bowl of batter kept cold over ice, long cooking chopsticks, a stonebow under the counter (+7, 1d6 + 4), a notebook of measurements, a thermometer of his own devising: a glass tube of colored spirit he calls the oil's pulse. Languages: Japanese (Edo and Nagasaki), a little Portuguese from his teacher, and the porters' slang of every road. The oil's pulse. A glass tube of colored spirit set in the oil, marked in his own hand. When it reaches the third mark, the fish goes in. Nobody else in Edo has one.

The notebook. Three hundred pages of batter, oil and weather. The last ten are about the stall across the bridge: what it sells, when, and who buys.

Background

A tempura seller of Edo who fries at a stall at the foot of Nihonbashi, because the city forbids frying indoors, and who claims his batter came from a Portuguese priest's cook at Nagasaki before the ban. He measures everything, writes it down and tests it again: the oil, the flour, the cold water, the weather. Porters and samurai eat off his skewers side by side, and he hears every rumor on the Tōkaidō. He wants to be the first in Edo to write the dish down properly. He is losing customers to a stall across the bridge whose fish is never out of season, even in winter, and whose owner nobody has seen in daylight.

How to play

  • He is a source of every rumor on the Tōkaidō: porters and samurai talk while they eat, and he is always listening.
  • He measures everything. Ask him what is strange about a place, and he will tell you its temperature, its smell and what changed last week.
  • A pot of hot oil is a fine Mechanism. Don't start a fight at his stall.
  • The hook is the stall across the bridge, and a good reason to be on a bridge at night.

Why this build

  • Why 5th: a cook who became a natural philosopher by measuring his oil: Experiments and Mechanism from a street stall.
  • Experimentalist: the batter is an experiment, and he runs it every night.
  • Artisan and Crafter for the stall; Skilled for the notebook and the listening.

One creature plus one object or feature at a time; studying another replaces the earlier one of its kind. A creature stays studied 1 minute; an object or feature until you finish a Short or Long Rest. Learn one fact. A creature: its AC, which save has the lowest bonus, one Resistance, Immunity, or Vulnerability (the DM chooses; or tells you it has none), or its Speeds and special senses. An object or feature: what it's made of, rough age, how it was made, or where it's weakest. Studying what you're already studying renews it and teaches another fact.
