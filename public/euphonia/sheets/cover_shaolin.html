Shi Yuanjing

Human · Acolyte · Monk 10 (Open Hand) · level 10
On the coverQing China, 1668Mount Song, Henan

Shaolin's fighting monks came badly through the fall of the Ming, and in 1668 they wait to see what the new dynasty wants with armed monasteries. The Qing Code has a statute against heterodox arts; a monastery that drills with staves is a question the governor hasn't asked yet. In Euphonia the discipline works: the body trained far enough becomes a power.

The bannermen came up the valley road to count the monks, and the abbot sent him down to meet them alone, with a staff and nothing else. The officer asked what the staff was for. He set its foot on the road, walked three steps up the face of the gate tower as if it were the road, and sat down on the lintel to wait for the next question.

Gear

A Shaolin staff of white wax-wood, eyebrow height (as a quarterstaff, +8, 1d8 + 4 with Martial Arts, Bludgeoning); gray robes and straw sandals; a string of 108 prayer beads; an alms bowl; a printed sutra in a cloth wrapper; a bundle of dried herbs and a bone-setter's splints; his ordination certificate from the last Ming years, which the Qing clerks have not yet asked to see. The Staff Manual. A hand-copied book of staff forms, the monastery's own, with the margins full of corrections in three hands. Not magic. While he has it, he can spend a Short Rest teaching a willing creature one staff form: until its next Long Rest, it can use a quarterstaff with the Topple mastery property. A novice who studies it a year learns to fall without breaking.

Background

Given to the monastery as a boy in a famine year, when the late Ming drought was emptying the north and a temple meant a bowl of millet. He learned to chant, to sweep, and to fight with the staff, because Shaolin's monks have been fighting with the staff since before the old men can remember; they fought the coast pirates for the Ming a century ago, and the monastery still keeps the memory. He was a young monk when the dynasty fell and the hills filled with deserters and rebels, and the monastery learned what it costs to be armed in a war you didn't choose. Now the Manchus hold Beijing, the emperor is fourteen, and the abbot sends him to speak with officials because he is calm, polite, and plainly impossible to arrest. He teaches the novices the staff. He has not decided what he will do if the governor tells him to stop.

How to play

  • Your Focus Points are the whole game, and they all come back on a Short Rest. Spend them: a Stunning Strike on the one that matters, a Flurry every turn the fight is close.
  • Open with Flurry of Blows and Topple: the bannerman on the ground has Disadvantage against everyone, and Push puts the next one off the wall.
  • Your speed is a weapon. Fifty feet, up the gate tower, across the pond, out of reach; Step of the Wind takes a novice with you.
  • You talk before you fight. Insight and Religion carry the monastery's business with the officials; a monk who can walk up a wall rarely needs to say so twice.
  • Self-Restoration ends fear and poison at the end of each of your turns, and Evasion takes the sting out of a volley. You are hard to stop.

Why this build

  • Level 10: a senior fighting monk, the one the abbot sends to the officials, not the abbot himself. Heightened Focus (three-strike Flurry) and Acrobatic Movement are the Shaolin of the stories.
  • Warrior of the Open Hand: the 2024 subclass of the pure martial art; Topple and Push are the staff's sweep and thrust. Shadow or Elements would be another monastery's style.
  • Acolyte (Wis +2, Int +1; Magic Initiate (Cleric)): he was raised in a temple. Alert (human Versatile): a monk who is never caught on the back foot.
  • Dexterity to 18 by two Ability Score Improvements, with Wisdom 16 from the start: AC 17 unarmored, Focus DC 15.
