Molière first played Tartuffe for the king at Versailles in 1664; the devout party had it banned within the week, and the Archbishop of Paris forbade the faithful, on pain of excommunication, to see it, read it or hear it read. The company plays it anyway in private houses for princes who don't care what the archbishop thinks, and the king lets it be known that he likes the play. The ban holds until early 1669.

Paris, the Palais-Royal. Molière has the king's patronage and half the devout of Paris against him; Tartuffe is banned, and he's playing it anyway in private houses. The courtier in the second row isn't watching the stage. She's watching the archbishop's secretary, who has started to laugh.

Background

A judge's widow of thirty-four who writes verse under three names and comedies under none, and goes to every private Tartuffe in Paris to watch the audience. Not the play: she knows it by heart. She keeps a list of the devout who have laughed at it, with the line that got them, and the list is long and getting longer, and nobody on it can preach against the play again without her mentioning which scene. She has never used it. She doesn't need to; the men on it know there is a list. Her own comedy, about a dévote who runs a charity for profit, has been read aloud in three salons and laughed at in all of them. She wants to see it played on a real stage under her own name, and she wants the archbishop's secretary to come.

How to play

  • She watches audiences. The Read and Every Table tell her who in the room has the power and who is hiding hostility; the list tells her who laughed.
  • The Aside: one sentence to one person across a crowded salon, and it lands like an hour's conversation.
  • The Masks: Columbine sees through a disguise at a masked ball, and the Pedant knows when a confessor is lying.
  • The Repertory: a company or a patron will hide her where plays aren't licensed, and get a party into a private performance nobody admits happened.
  • The hook is her comedy. A stage, a licence or a word to the right actress would make her a friend for life; a dévote who learns what the play is about would make her an enemy.

Why this build

  • Why 6th: four years of private Tartuffes, and The Aside: the line that lands on one person in a crowded room.
  • College of Verse: the Verse college's opening vignette, the courtier at a private Tartuffe watching the archbishop's secretary laugh (The Classes, Courtier, Verse).
  • Noble for a judge's widow of the robe; Alert, because she sees the room before the room sees her.
