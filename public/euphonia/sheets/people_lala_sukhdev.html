Multani bankers, Hindu moneylenders from the Punjab, ran a credit network across Central Asia, Persia and Russia; hundreds lived in Bukhara's caravanserais in the 17th century, lending to herders, khans and students and writing bills honored back in India. They typically left their families at home in Multan.

An upper room in the Indian caravanserai at Bukhara, carpets, ledgers and a pot of tea. A plump man in a white turban counts a herder's loan onto a cloth, coin by coin, twice, so the herder can follow, and writes the sum in a ledger the herder can't read and trusts anyway. He looks up at you, smiles, and asks how far you live from Multan, because that is how he sets his rate.

Background

A Multani moneylender, one of some five hundred Indian bankers in Bukhara's caravanserais, who lends to herders, khans and madrasa students at rates set by how far they live from Multan. He counts every loan onto a cloth twice so the borrower can follow, and is trusted by men who trust no one else. He'll advance a party anything against a bill on Surat. The Bukhara buyer who wants Nurbibi's live beast writes his offers through him, and he is not sure he likes it. He wants to go home to Multan for his daughter's wedding without losing his clients to his cousin.

How to play

  • He is a patron or a contact: he'll advance a party anything against a bill on Surat.
  • Follow the Money: every loan on the steppe crosses his carpet, and he knows who owes whom.
  • He is honest and careful, and his rate is a judgment of how far you are from home.
  • The hook is his daughter's wedding, and his cousin. Help him go home without losing his clients.

Why this build

  • Why 8th: twenty years on a carpet in Bukhara, and a name honored from Multan to Astrakhan.
  • Factor: a banking house, and a steppe full of borrowers.
  • Merchant and Lucky; Skilled for the languages of everyone who borrows.
