The extirpation of idolatries: visitors-general sent by the Archbishop of Lima to Andean parishes to find and burn the mallquis, the mummified ancestors, and the huacas, the stones and places that speak. Holiness works for anyone actually holy here, and his does; the huacas answer their keepers just as plainly. This is a hard part of the real record, and a table chooses how much of it to use.

A highland plaza, cold at noon. A gentle, gray-haired priest sits at a table with a notary on one side and a ledger of every family's ancestors open in front of him. Soldiers stand behind. A bonfire is laid, not yet lit. He calls the names in a kind voice, one by one, and writes down who doesn't answer. A child brings him water. He thanks her by name.

The ledger. Every family in the parish, and its ancestors by name, taken from confessions. The villagers know what it is for.

Background

A visitor-general of idolatries for the Archbishop of Lima. He is sincere, learned and kind to children, and he has burned more mallquis than any man living. His prayers work, which he takes as proof. Holiness works for anyone actually holy, and the huacas answer their keepers just as plainly, which frightens him more than he admits. He writes long letters to Rome on the question and never sends them. He wants one village, just one, where the stone stays silent after he has prayed over it.

How to play

  • He is a villain who believes he is saving souls, and he is kind. The table should find that harder than cruelty.
  • His prayers work, and so do the huacas'. Every scene with him puts two answers side by side.
  • He is Frightened. A party that shows him a village where the stone speaks after he has prayed may break him, or make him worse.
  • Use him lightly or heavily; the visitations are a hard part of the record.

Why this build

  • Why 9th: twenty years of visitations. Dispel Evil and Good is the prayer he says over the stones.
  • War Domain, as the roster has him: the Church militant, with soldiers at his back and a bonfire laid.
  • Protector, for a man who goes into the mountains with a soldier's kit; Acolyte for Salamanca.
